**Course Overview**
The Introduction to Quantitative Methods in Finance course is designed to provide participants with fundamentals of probabilistic techniques applied in option markets. Course Description This course delves into the application of mathematical principles in the domain of option markets through Python. The curriculum commences with an overview of probability theory and probabilistic measurement techniques. Moving forward, the course delves into the mathematical foundation of stock movements as Brownian motions. The concept of volatility is examined, enabling participants to model and predict market fluctuations. The course then shifts its focus to option trading, covering European options and introducing the Black-Scholes model. Risk management of option strategies are introduced through the study of the Greeks, or measurements which quantify option price sensitivity. The technique of delta hedging is thoroughly dissected as a means to manage risk exposure in practical trading scenarios and to create expected profit distributions that are more favorable to the investor and reduce a portfolio’s risk. Monte Carlo simulation is introduced as a tool for option pricing and risk assessment. Participants will be introduced how to utilize this computational method effectively and 1 efficiently through vectorized code, with an additional emphasis on control variates through delta hedging to enhance accuracy. This course is tailored for individuals transitioning to roles within quantitative finance and offers a pragmatic blend of theoretical insights and hands-on problem-solving exercises through Python. It equips participants with introductory methods used by proprietary trading firms. Student Learning Outcomes By the end of this course, students will be able to:
- Use probabilistic methodology to study stock movement volatility using historical data.
- Create accurate European option price predictions by enhancing Monte-Carlo simulations through the use of control variates. Accuracy will be measured against closed form solutions to option contracts provided by Black-Scholes option pricing equations.
- Adjust Monte-Carlo simulations of European contracts for the purpose of pricing more complicated option contracts whose expected value does not have a closed form solution. Such option contracts may include American, Asian, lookback, and barrier option contracts.
- Develop hedging strategies which minimize risk and improve expected profit distributions of an option investment strategy.
- Develop independently designed stock volatility models for the purpose of creating hedging strategies that protect profits gained through the sell and purchases of derivative based financial instruments such as options, futures, and bonds.

Mini-Project “3.5” This was not an officially assigned mini-project, but was based on some of my own independent exploration using my background in linear algebra. In the boot camp lectures, we considered the effect of periodic Delta-hedging on an option position. In order to simulate a periodically hedged option, we need to generate random paths followed by the underlying stock price, not just its values at the option’s expiration. If we need to simulate the values of the stock at times t1 < t2 < · · · < tn, one approach (easily done in numpy in a vectorized way) is incremental path generation: draw the first value St1 , then draw St2 conditional on the already-drawn St1 , and so on. An alternative is to draw the entire path at once, which can be done ERDOS QUANT FINANCE BOOT CAMP MINI-PROJECTS SUMMARY 3 ˝ since we know what the covariance matrix C of St1 , . . . , Stn must be. The necessary construction is a pseudo square root of C, that is, a matrix A such that AAT = C. Such an A is not unique; there is a unique lower triangular choice of A (AAT in this case is known as a Cholesky decomposition), which matches what is done in the incremental path generation method, but there are other possibilities. For example, if C = P DP T is an orthogonal diagonalization of the symmetric matrix C, then A = P D1/2 is another pseudo square root of C that can be used to generate stock price paths. One advantage of this method is that if the diagonal entries of D (which are the eigenvalues of C) are listed in decreasing order, then the transform formulas used to generate the stock price paths take the form of summations where the terms at the end are weighted less heavily. “Forgetting” a few of these terms (truncating the sum) results in a simulation of a lower-dimensional problem that converges more quickly. In the mini-project, I performed Monte Carlo simulations to price an Asian option whose payoff at expiration is based on the average of the stock price at several different times using three methods: incremental path generation, orthogonal diagonalization, and the truncated variant. I also compared the run times of these simulations as well as their rates of convergence. 5. Mini-Project 4: The Black-Scholes model in quantitative finance is a highly idealized model for the price of European call and put options. One of its assumptions is that the stock price follows a geometric Brownian motion; this includes the assumption of having constant volatility. Under this assumption, one can derive the price for European calls and puts in a way that is independent of spot price. In general, a trade in options without a dependence on spot price is called delta-hedging, where the delta ∆ of an option is the sensitivity of the option to spot price. One can then hedge against the effect of ∆ on the option by trading in a suitable quantity of the underlying asset. Indeed, the formulas for the option prices in the Black-Scholes model are derived from the assumption that one can continuously delta-hedge. The Heston model is a generalization of the Black-Scholes model in which we allow for non-constant volatility. In this project we provide some simulations comparing the strategies of delta-hedging in the Black-Scholes model and the Heston model, by plotting the distributions of the resulting profits and losses. This way, we see how the non-constant volatility in the Heston model affects the profit/loss distribution. Finally, we provide some simulations to vega-hedge in the Heston model. This strategy involves hedging against the vega ν, which is precisely the sensitivity of the option price to the volatility. We then compare the effects of delta-hedging in the Black-Scholes model with vega+delta-hedging in the Heston model.
